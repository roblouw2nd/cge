Google Ads for medical practices.

Google Ads works for medical practices because patients search with clear intent, but it has to be run differently from other local services. Google restricts how health advertisers can target people, and in the US a practice can't send patient information to ad or analytics platforms the way a plumber sends leads. The accounts that do well are built on search intent and location, measured with conversion tracking that carries no patient data, and judged on booked appointments, not form fills.

I've run Google Ads, tracking and consulting for a medical practice in New York State for three and a half years. This is the approach, with the usual caveat up front: I'm an engineer, not a lawyer. Treat the compliance sections as a briefing for the conversation with your own counsel.

What Google won't let health advertisers do

Three policy areas shape everything.

No personalised targeting on health. Google's personalised advertising policy doesn't allow targeting people based on health conditions. In practice that means no remarketing lists for most medical services, and limits on audience targeting generally. An account plan that depends on "retarget everyone who visited the treatment page" won't get approved, and shouldn't.

Restricted categories. Some areas need certification or aren't allowed at all, depending on country: prescription drugs, addiction services, certain unproven treatments. Check the healthcare and medicines policy for your specialty before building anything.

Claims. Ads and landing pages that promise outcomes, or use before-and-after framing carelessly, attract disapprovals. Write plainly about what you treat and how to book.

None of this stops a practice advertising. It just means the account leans on the things that are allowed: what someone typed, where they are, and when.

Tracking without sending patient data

This is where most practice accounts are either reckless or blind.

The reckless version: a standard pixel-and-analytics setup copied from an ecommerce site, firing on every page including appointment forms and patient portals, passing along page URLs that name conditions together with identifiers. US regulators have made clear they consider some uses of online tracking on healthcare sites a HIPAA problem, and Google itself says Analytics must not be used to collect protected health information and offers no business associate agreement for it.

The blind version: a practice so worried about the above that it tracks nothing, and has no idea which campaigns produce patients.

The middle path I build looks like this:

  • Tags only on public marketing pages. Nothing on portals, nothing behind a login.
  • Conversions that say "a booking request happened" and nothing more. No condition, no free-text field contents, no names or emails passed to the ad platform.
  • Generic confirmation URLs. A thank-you page shouldn't have the treatment or the patient in its address.
  • Phone calls counted as calls. For many practices calls are the majority of bookings. Count them, by duration threshold, without recording content into ad systems.
  • The patient record stays in systems covered by the right agreements. Reconciliation between ad data and booked appointments happens on the practice's side, in aggregate.
  • Consent handled properly, and the whole setup written down so your compliance adviser can review it.

If your current setup was installed by a generalist agency, it's worth checking against the tracking health check and then against that list. The broader approach to owning your measurement is in the note on first-party tracking.

The ad platform needs to know that a booking happened. It never needs to know who, or for what.

Structure the account around how patients search

One campaign per service line. A new-patient general appointment, a specific procedure and an urgent same-week visit are different searches with different values. Blending them makes automated bidding average across patients who aren't alike. This is the same principle I use in self-storage and chauffeur accounts, and it matters more here because targeting options are narrower.

Tight geography. Patients travel a limited distance. Set location targeting to people in the area, not people "interested in" it, and draw the radius from where existing patients come from.

Ad scheduling around the front desk. If calls go to voicemail after five, call-focused ads after five are waste. Either staff the phone, offer online booking, or schedule accordingly.

Insurance and eligibility in the ad. If you don't take a given plan, or only see adults, say so. A click from someone you can't treat is the most expensive kind.

Where practice budgets leak

The patterns I see most in healthcare accounts:

Job seekers and students. Searches containing "jobs", "salary", "residency", "course" and "certification" trigger medical keywords constantly. They need to be negatives from day one.

Existing patients clicking brand ads. People searching the practice's name to find the phone number or the portal login. Worth protecting the brand term, but those clicks aren't new patients and shouldn't be reported as acquisition.

Free and informational searches. "Symptoms of", "what causes", "home remedy". High volume, low booking intent.

Out-of-area traffic. The default location setting includes people merely interested in your area.

The waste finder will put a rough figure on these in a couple of minutes. The real figure is in your search terms report.

Measure booked appointments, not form fills

A request form is a lead. A patient who attends is the outcome. The gap between them is where practices fool themselves: a campaign can produce plenty of requests from people who are out of network, out of area, or never answer the call back.

The fix is the same as in any lead-quality account, adapted for privacy. The practice tracks which requests became booked appointments in its own system. That result goes back to the ad platform as an offline conversion keyed to the ad click identifier, with no patient details attached, or it's reconciled in aggregate each month if your counsel prefers that nothing goes back at all. Either way, budget decisions get made on appointments.

That's the approach in a paragraph: campaigns by service line, tracking that tells Google a booking happened and nothing else, and regular reconciliation against the practice's own numbers.
