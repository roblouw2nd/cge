When not to hire a fractional growth engineer.

Don't hire a fractional growth engineer if you haven't yet proved people want the product, if your ad spend is too small for senior time to pay back, if what you need is brand, creative or team leadership, or if you already have a full-time job's worth of defined execution. In those cases a different purchase is better, and often cheaper. I do this work for a living, and these are the cases where I'd tell you not to hire me, with what to buy instead.

If you're not sure what the role is in the first place, start here.

1. You haven't proved anyone wants it yet

Growth engineering makes a working thing work better. It measures demand accurately, buys more of it efficiently and converts more of it. It doesn't create demand for a product nobody has bought. If you have no customers, or only customers from your personal network, the bottleneck is the offer, and no amount of tracking will fix an offer.

Instead: sell it by hand to ten strangers. Then call.

2. The spend is too small for the fee to pay back

My fee has to be small against the money it improves. If you spend a few hundred a month on ads, even a large percentage improvement is a small number, and a recurring senior day rate would cost more than the whole ad budget. That's bad buying and I'd be embarrassed to invoice it.

Instead: a one-off fixed-fee audit or tracking setup, then run it yourself. Or use the free tools: the waste finder and tracking health check will get a small account most of the way.

3. What you need is brand or creative

If the problem is that nobody knows who you are, or the positioning is muddled, or you need a campaign idea people remember, that's brand and creative work. I can tell you accurately how the campaign performed. I'm not who you want writing it.

Instead: a brand strategist or creative agency. Bring in measurement afterwards so you can tell whether it worked.

4. You need someone to lead a marketing team

If you have four marketers and no one setting priorities, hiring, or representing marketing at the leadership table, that's a leadership gap. A builder working one day a week can't fill it and shouldn't try.

Instead: a fractional CMO. I've written up how the two roles differ and which to hire first in growth engineer vs fractional CMO.

5. There's already forty hours a week of defined work

Fractional fits the stage where the work is senior, varied and part-time. Once the playbook is proven and the job is running it at volume every day, fractional becomes an expensive way to buy hours. A good fractional operator should tell you when you've reached that point and help you hire the replacement.

Instead: a full-time hire, or agency capacity if the work is production. The ROI calculator will show you roughly where the lines cross, and it does say so when fractional loses.

6. Nobody can act on what gets built

This one is less obvious. I can build tracking that shows which leads become customers, but someone has to mark leads as qualified in the CRM. I can prove a landing page change would lift conversion, but someone has to approve it. If the business has no one with the time or authority to act on findings, the engagement produces accurate reports that nobody uses.

Instead: fix the ownership question first. Name one person who owns the weekly number. Then the build has somewhere to land.

7. You want someone to blame

Sometimes the real brief is "the board wants to see we did something about marketing". An outside operator makes a convenient answer to that. But if the appetite to change budgets, pages or process isn't there, nothing will move, and in six months the story will be that the consultant didn't work.

Instead: be honest about it internally. If the answer is that nothing will change, save the money.

The best outcome of a first call is sometimes "not yet". It costs you thirty minutes and saves a quarter.

And when it is the right call

The fit is narrow but common. You have real demand and real ad spend. You can't confidently say which campaigns make money. Work keeps falling between an agency and your own to-do list. You need senior hands now and can't justify the full-time hire yet. If that's you, the engagement usually pays for itself from the waste it removes, before any growth.
