Fractional growth engineer vs growth agency.

A fractional growth engineer is one senior operator who builds your growth system hands-on, inside your own accounts, a day or two a week, and hands it over. A growth agency is a team that runs channels for you on a monthly retainer, usually with an account manager between you and the people doing the work. The first sells senior build work. The second sells managed capacity. Most of the frustration I hear from founders comes from buying one when they needed the other.

I'm obviously not neutral here, so I'll do what I did in the pricing note and include the cases where the agency is the right buy. There are several.

Cost: similar fee, different composition

At one day a week, a senior fractional engagement and a mid-market agency retainer often land in the same range. What you get for the money is different. An agency fee pays for the work, plus account management, plus tooling, plus a share of the agency's sales and overhead. A fractional fee pays for the work. Neither is a scandal. It's just how each business is built.

The pricing structure matters more than the headline number. A retainer priced as a percentage of ad spend pays the agency more when you spend more, whether or not the extra spend was a good idea. A day rate or a fixed fee doesn't move when your budget does, so the advice you get about budget is cleaner. Speed: fewer handoffs, fewer hours

One operator working across the whole system is fast at exactly the things agencies are slow at. If a form fires its conversion on click instead of on success, I can find it, fix the tag, correct the conversion action in Google Ads and adjust the bid strategy in one sitting. In an agency that's a ticket to the tracking team, a note to the web developer and a follow-up call, and each of those has its own queue.

The honest other side: one person has one person's hours. If you need forty ad variants a week across four platforms, a team will out-produce me every time. Speed of decision favours the single operator. Speed of production favours the agency.

Ownership: the question to ask before you sign

Ask any vendor one thing: when we stop working together, what do I keep?

The right answer is everything. The ad accounts are yours and you hold admin access. The tag manager containers, the analytics properties, the landing pages, the dashboards and the documentation are yours. A fractional engagement is designed around that outcome, because the job is to build something your team can run without me. My longest client relationship is five years and ongoing, and they could fire me tomorrow and lose nothing but the operator.

Plenty of agencies work this way too. Some don't. If campaigns live in the agency's own ad account, or reporting lives in a tool only they can log into, then you're renting a system, and the exit cost is a hidden line item on every invoice.

The fee is the visible cost. The exit cost is the one to price before you sign.

When the agency is the right buy

Three situations, plainly. Volume: large creative production or always-on content, where the work is hours, not judgment. Breadth: five or six channels running at once, each needing a specialist. Brand: a big campaign idea, a rebrand, a launch film. None of that is growth engineering and a senior day rate is a bad way to buy it.

There's also a hybrid that works well: the engineer builds the measurement and the account structure, then an agency or an in-house junior runs the weekly production on top of it. The agency's output gets better because the conversion data underneath it is finally true.

Where "embedded growth pods" fit

A newer agency model sells an embedded pod: a small team (often a strategist, a media buyer and a designer) that joins your Slack and works as if in-house. It solves the account-manager problem and gives you more hands than one operator can. It's still managed capacity on a subscription, though, and the same ownership question applies. A pod suits a company with a working machine that needs more throughput. If the machine doesn't exist yet, or the numbers coming out of it can't be trusted, more hands just produce more of the wrong thing. That's also the short version of the growth engineer vs fractional CMO question: build the instruments before you hire people to play them.

Four questions that decide it

1. Is the problem building or running? If tracking is broken, attribution is a guess or the site doesn't convert, you need a builder. If the system works and needs feeding, you need capacity.

2. Can you trust your numbers today? If platform conversions don't reconcile with revenue, fix that first, whoever you hire. The tracking health check takes two minutes and will tell you how bad it is.

3. How many hours a week does the work really need? Under about fifteen senior hours, fractional fits. Over forty hours of defined execution, hire full-time or buy agency capacity.

4. Who will own the system in a year? If the answer is "us", choose the model that hands it over.
