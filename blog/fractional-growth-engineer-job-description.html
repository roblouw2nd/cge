Fractional growth engineer job description.

A fractional growth engineer job description should describe a builder: a senior, part-time operator who is responsible for making paid acquisition, conversion tracking, the website and reporting work as one system, and for handing that system to your team. If your draft reads like a strategy role, with words like "oversee", "align" and "own the roadmap", you're describing a fractional CMO, which is a different hire.

I do this job, so I've read a lot of briefs for it. Most are either too vague to screen against or a wish list for three people. Below is a template you can copy and edit, followed by what to cut and how to interview against it.

The template

Title: Fractional Growth Engineer (part-time, contract)

Commitment: 1 to 2 days per week, initial 3-month term, remote.

The role in one paragraph. We spend [amount] a month on paid acquisition and can't yet say with confidence which of it produces revenue. We need a senior operator to build the system that answers that question and then improves the answer: accurate conversion tracking, a well-structured ad account optimised to qualified outcomes, landing pages that convert, and a weekly number the leadership team trusts. You'll do the work yourself, in our accounts, and document it so our team can run it.

Responsibilities.

  • Audit our current ad accounts, tracking and funnel in the first two weeks and give us a written, prioritised fix-list.
  • Implement or repair conversion tracking end to end, including server-side or offline conversion imports where they're justified, and reconcile platform numbers against our CRM or revenue data.
  • Restructure and manage paid campaigns ([Google Ads / Meta / LinkedIn]) to optimise for qualified leads or revenue, not clicks or raw form fills.
  • Build or change landing pages, forms and quote or booking flows directly, without waiting on a development queue.
  • Set up one weekly report: spend, qualified outcomes, cost per qualified outcome, by channel.
  • Document everything and train the person who'll own it.

Must have.

  • Several years of hands-on paid media management with budgets similar to ours. Hands-on means in the account weekly, not supervising someone who is.
  • Has personally implemented conversion tracking: tag management, consent handling, enhanced or offline conversions.
  • Can edit a web page, a form and a tag without help.
  • Can show a system built for a past client that the client still runs.

Nice to have. Experience in our industry. Server-side tagging. SQL or a warehouse. CRM integration work.

What success looks like at 90 days. Platform-reported conversions reconcile with our own records within a tolerance we've agreed. We know cost per qualified lead by campaign. Wasted spend identified in the audit has been cut. At least one conversion improvement has shipped and been measured.

Ownership. All accounts, containers, pages and documentation are created in and remain in our ownership.

What to leave out

The briefs that fail usually fail by addition. Things I'd cut:

Brand strategy, positioning and PR. That's marketing leadership. Asking for it here gets you a generalist who does neither job well.

Managing a team. A day a week isn't enough to manage people and build systems. Pick one.

High-volume content or creative production. A senior day rate is an expensive way to buy forty social posts. Hire a junior or an agency for volume, and let the engineer tell them what's working. I've written more on that split in growth engineer vs growth agency.

A list of twelve tools. Tools change. Screen for the ability to make platform numbers match reality, in whatever stack you have.

How to screen against it

The title is young, so anyone can wear it. These questions sort builders from advisers quickly.

"Walk me through the last conversion tracking setup you built yourself." You're listening for specifics: what fired where, what broke, how they verified it. "I worked with the dev team to" is an adviser's answer.

"Show me something you built that a client still uses without you." A quote tool, a store, a tracking setup, a reporting pipeline. Builders have these. Mine include an instant quote estimator for a chauffeur operator and a B2B store that's been running for five years.

"Our Google Ads says 120 leads last month and our CRM says 70. Where do you look first?" A good answer has a sequence: duplicate firing, conversions counted on click instead of submit, counting settings, attribution windows, spam, then offline matching. If you want to know what a good answer sounds like, this note covers the usual causes.

"What happens to everything when we stop working together?" The only right answer: it's yours, in your accounts, documented.

"When would you tell us not to hire you?" Someone senior will have a ready answer. I've written mine down in when not to hire a fractional growth engineer.

Set the price expectation before you post

Expect a senior day rate, a fixed fee for a defined build, or a monthly programme fee. Rates vary a lot by market, which is why I point people at a calculator with editable assumptions instead of quoting one number.
