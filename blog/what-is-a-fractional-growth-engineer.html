What is a fractional growth engineer? (And when to hire one.)

A fractional growth engineer is a senior operator who builds a company's growth systems hands-on — paid acquisition, conversion tracking, the website, and the data that ties them together — on a part-time or project basis, and then hands the working system to your team. The word doing the work in that sentence is builds. Not advises, not oversees, not aligns. Builds.

The title is my answer to a gap I kept meeting in client work: companies that had plenty of strategy and no working machinery. They'd had the agency deck, sometimes a fractional CMO, occasionally a junior in-house marketer — and still couldn't answer the only question that matters: which of the money we spend actually makes us money?

What the work actually looks like.

Concretely, across my current and recent engagements, the work has meant: rebuilding a Google Ads programme around lead quality for the Middle East's largest self-storage provider and implementing server-side tracking behind it; building an instant quote estimator and booking platform for a luxury chauffeur operator in Ireland and the UK (its fleet tripled over our three years); wiring SevenRooms booking data into GA4 for an upmarket international restaurant group so their paid social was judged on covers, not clicks; running compliant patient acquisition for a New York medical practice; and taking a B2B PPE supplier from no online presence to a seven-figure online store over five years.

Notice the pattern: every one of those is a system — ads, tracking, web and data working as one machine — not a channel run in isolation.

Fractional growth engineer vs fractional CMO.

A fractional CMO leads: strategy, positioning, brand, hiring, budget. A fractional growth engineer ships: campaigns, tracking infrastructure, integrations, stores, quote platforms. Both are legitimate. But most growth-stage companies I meet don't yet have anything for a CMO to orchestrate — the instruments aren't installed. You can't conduct an orchestra that hasn't got instruments.

Hire the CMO when the machine exists and needs a driver. Hire the growth engineer when the machine needs building.

Fractional growth engineer vs agency.

An agency gives you a team — which in practice means an account manager between you and the work, seniors in the pitch and juniors in the account. A fractional growth engineer gives you one senior person doing the work directly, across the whole system, with no handoffs. The trade-off is honest: an agency scales hours better; a single operator can't be in ten accounts a day. But if the work is senior, varied and part-time by nature — which growth engineering at this stage is — the single-operator model wins on both quality and cost. My longest client relationship is five years and ongoing; retention like that is the model working, not a sales claim.

Fractional growth engineer vs full-time hire.

Hire full-time when there's 40+ hours a week of proven playbook to execute. Before that point, a full-time senior growth hire is expensive idle capacity — and a junior one can't build the system alone. Fractional fits the in-between stage: senior skill, part-time shape. What it costs.

Typically a day rate, one to two days a week, or a fixed project fee. At one day a week the annual cost usually lands well below half the fully loaded cost of a senior full-time hire, and below most full-service agency retainers. The structural difference matters more than the fee: you're paying for senior execution, not account management overhead.

When it's the right call.

Three signals, any one of which is enough. First: you're spending real money on ads and can't confidently say which campaigns produce revenue — that's a tracking problem, and it's upstream of everything. Second: growth work keeps falling between your agency and your own to-do list — strategy exists, nothing ships. Third: you need senior execution now but can't justify (or find) the full-time hire yet.

And when it isn't: if you need brand repositioning, a big creative campaign, or someone to manage a five-person marketing team, hire the CMO or the agency. Growth engineering is the machinery layer. It's the layer I'd fix first — but it isn't every layer.

Questions worth asking anyone with this title.

The title is young enough that anyone can wear it, so interrogate the "engineer" part: Will you build the tracking yourself, or "coordinate" it? Show me a system you built that the client still runs without you. What happens to the campaigns, containers and dashboards when we part ways? (Right answer: they're yours, documented, in your accounts.) Vague answers to any of these means you're talking to a consultant with a rebranded title — which is fine, but it's a different purchase.
